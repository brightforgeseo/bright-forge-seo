Hiring an SEO specialist is a procurement problem with a technical test attached. The Philippine market has capable independents, mixed teams, and a long tail of packages that cannot work as described. The way to tell them apart is not a certificate wall. It is whether the person can look at your live URLs, name a problem you recognise, and describe work you could inspect in 90 days.

Start with the work, not with a job title. Crawl issues, thin commercial pages, weak local listings, messy migrations and enquiry paths that leak are different jobs. A specialist who is excellent at one of those can still be a poor hire for the rest. If you hire the title, you will get the title's favourite activity. If you hire against a named problem, you can test for it.

Keep every account in the business's name from the first week. Search Console, analytics, the CMS, hosting, the domain, ads and Google Business Profile. A specialist who needs those logins to work is normal. A specialist who needs to own them is not.

## Write The Job Before You Write The Ad

Name the commercial outcome. Calls, forms, booked jobs, store visits, or qualified sales conversations. Those outcomes live on different pages. A clinic that needs appointment requests is not the same hire as a manufacturer that needs spec-sheet enquiries.

Name the geography and the language. Metro Manila only, nationwide from Cebu, overseas buyers reading English, local buyers reading Filipino, or a mix. Specialists who have only shipped English brochure sites will still apply. Your brief should make that a conversation, not a surprise in month two.

Name the pages. Homepage, money services, location or category templates, and any URL the sales team already sends. If an important page is missing, write that down. Do not hire someone to rank a query that has no URL.

Name the constraints. CMS vendor lock, developer days per month, legal review, claims you cannot make, and whether prices can be published. Constraints are not embarrassing. They are the job.

Name what has already been tried. A blog programme, a redesign, a previous retainer, a link campaign, a tool subscription. Give the outcome in plain terms. Traffic moved and enquiries did not. Rankings dropped after the redesign. The last audit was never implemented. Specialists should not discover your history in a backlink export.

## Choose A Shape Of Help, Then A Person

A freelancer is strongest on a bounded task. Technical cleanup on a site that already converts, a rewrite of a small set of service pages, a Business Profile rebuild, or a map of queries for an in-house writer. You are buying a specialist hour and a direct conversation.

An agency is stronger when several skills have to run in sequence, when someone must cover leave, and when commercial accountability matters more than a single craft. You are buying a delivery system. That system costs more because it contains more than one person.

In-house is a third shape. It works when there is enough work every week to fill a role, and when that person can get CMS and developer time without begging. It fails when the hire is expected to be strategist, writer, developer and reporter, then measured on rankings they do not control.

[Freelance SEO versus an agency in the Philippines](/blog/freelance-seo-vs-an-agency-in-the-philippines/) is the capacity comparison. Make that choice before you fall in love with a CV. A brilliant freelancer asked to be an agency will drop workstreams. An agency asked to do a two day cleanup will still try to sell a year.

White label sits to the side. Another firm may already be selling SEO to your customers and looking for delivery. That can be a clean specialist hire if the accounts stay with the end client and the reporting is inspectable. It is a bad hire if you never meet the people doing the work.

## How To Test Diagnosis On Your Site

Send two or three live URLs and a short brief. Ask what they would look at first and why. You are not asking for free labour on a full audit. You are asking whether they can see.

A useful reply names a page, a possible blocker, and a way to confirm it. Title is vague. Internal links from the wrong template. A form below unrelated content. A listing that disagrees with the footer. A cluster of near duplicate location pages. Specificity is the test.

A weak reply is a package. Bronze, Silver, Gold. A list of tools they use. A guarantee. A claim that they have a special relationship with Google. Those answers can be pasted into any thread. They tell you the specialist does not need your URLs.

Ask what they would refuse. Bulk links, ranking guarantees, mass city pages, content the brand cannot stand behind. Refusal is a method. A specialist with no refusals will do whatever the last client paid for.

Ask who does the work. If you are hiring a named specialist, that person should appear in the delivery, not only on the call. If you are hiring a team, you should know who crawls, who writes, and who talks to your developer.

Ask for a live example of work like yours. Open the URL. Look at the service page, not at a slide. You will not get every client named, and you should not demand confidential analytics. You can demand that the public work exists.

## Interviews That Separate Operators From Salespeople

Ask them to walk through Search Console on a site they can show, even a personal project or a redacted view. If they cannot narrate coverage, queries and pages, they will not narrate yours.

Ask what they would do in month one on your site, given the constraints you already sent. Listen for sequence. Indexation before content volume. Conversion path on money URLs before a new blog idea. Local consistency before a review stunt.

Ask how they measure a good month. Shipped URLs, Search Console movement on commercial queries, and enquiry quality are adult answers. Rankings for vanity terms and impression spikes on irrelevant queries are not.

Ask how they handle a developer queue. Specialists who have only worked on sites they fully control will stall on yours. You need someone who can write a ticket a developer will accept, and who can live with a two week release without filling the gap with junk posts.

Ask what happens when they are wrong. A ranking drop after a release, a page that does not move, a listing suspended. You want a diagnostic habit, not a defence of the last invoice.

Do not run a trivia contest about algorithm names. Operators talk about pages, templates and evidence. Salespeople talk about updates as weather.

## Contracts, Access And The First 90 Days

The contract should define work, not positions. Discovery, the first diagnosis, production limits, reporting artefacts, and what is out of scope. Ranking guarantees do not belong in the document. Forecast language can, if it is labelled as judgement.

Access should be listed. Owner on Search Console. Access to analytics. CMS role that can publish or at least stage. Business Profile Manager. Staging if it exists. If you cannot grant those, the specialist can still work from the public site, and the contract should say the diagnosis will be thinner.

Ownership should be boring. Domain, hosting, CMS, ads and listings stay with the business. Content they write should be yours to keep. Reports should be exportable. If leaving means losing the site, you did not hire a specialist. You rented a landlord.

Payment should follow the unit of work. A one-off diagnosis is a project fee. A campaign is a retainer. A burst of senior time is days. Mixing those units in one vague monthly number is how you pay forever for a cleanup that never ends.

The first 90 days are a fairness window for whether the work is real, not a deadline for winning the channel. You should see shipped changes, a written diagnosis, and reporting you can check without the vendor in the room. You should not expect a competitive city term to be settled. Anyone who collapses those two clocks is selling speed.

## Red Flags That End The Process

Guaranteed first-page rankings, especially with a refund clause so wide that any client delay voids it.

Link packages priced by the hundred, or any pitch that treats referring domains as inventory.

Refusal to add you on Search Console, or reporting only from a private tracker.

Keeping the CMS, domain or ads account in their name.

Content you are not allowed to edit, or writing that cannot be traced to a person who will take a brief.

A strategy that never names a URL by the end of discovery.

"AI SEO" with no plan for crawlability, proof, or the pages that would need to be cited.

Pressure to sign before they have seen the site. Urgency is a sales tool. Diagnosis is not urgent in that way.

## After You Hire, Manage The Work Like Operations

Give one internal owner. Not a committee. Someone who can approve copy, chase the developer, and read the monthly note.

Meet the reporting with questions about URLs and enquiries, not about whether the chart is up and to the right. If six weeks pass with no URL you can load and see a difference, the hire is already failing, even if the PDF is pretty.

Protect the specialist from random extra channels. Asking the same person to run ads, social and email because "it is all digital" is how SEO becomes a leftover. If you need those, hire them separately or accept that the SEO hours will shrink.

Review the brief quarterly. Offers change. Cities change. A page that was money in January may be a dead service in June. Specialists cannot guess that from Search Console alone.

Fire on method, not on impatience. If the work is real and the market is slow, three quiet months can still be the right path. If the work is theatre, do not wait for a year to confirm it. Take the accounts, export the files, and stop the residue.
