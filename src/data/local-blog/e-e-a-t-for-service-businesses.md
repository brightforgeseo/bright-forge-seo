Service businesses keep asking for an E-E-A-T score. There is not one. Google uses Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trust as a quality framework for human raters, then trains automated systems to prefer pages that look like they came from a real operator. You cannot buy a badge. You can make it obvious that a named person did the work, can be reached, and will not invent the result.

That is the whole job for a plumber, a law firm, an HVAC company, a clinic, or an SEO agency. The searcher is buying a job they cannot inspect before they hire. The site either looks like a firm that has done the job, or it looks like a brochure that could sit on any domain.

## What E-E-A-T Actually Means

Experience is first-hand involvement. The page should show that someone used the product, ran the process, stood on the site, or delivered the service. Screenshots of a Search Console coverage report, a crawl of a broken canonical, or a method written from a live account beat a paragraph that says we are experienced.

Expertise is command of the subject. For a service firm that is usually trade knowledge, not a pile of certificates. A drainage company that explains why a particular pipe run fails after heavy rain is showing expertise. A generic “we are experts in drainage solutions” line is not.

Authoritativeness is whether other people treat you as a source worth citing. It is mostly earned off your own site: mentions, links, reviews, association listings, and repeat work in a defined market. You cannot schema your way into it.

Trust is the centre of the four. Google has said so in the quality rater guidelines and in public Search Central writing. An untrustworthy page rates poorly even if the writer sounds senior. Fake reviews, hidden owners, a contact form that goes nowhere, and invented case studies destroy trust faster than a thin bio.

E-E-A-T itself is not a ranking factor you can score. Raters do not push a slider that changes your positions overnight. They label examples so Google can check whether its systems are surfacing helpful results. The practical implication is still blunt: if a careful human cannot tell who you are, what you have done, and how to reach you, the page is weak on the same qualities those systems are trying to detect.

## Why Service Businesses Feel This First

A product listing can show a photograph, a specification and a return policy. A service offer on the web is a promise about work that happens later, often in someone else’s building, with money already committed. Searchers know that. Quality raters know that. The gap between a real operator and a lead-gen shell is obvious once you look for it.

We see the same pattern on client sites and on our own. The homepage claims twenty years in the trade. The about copy names nobody. The blog is “Admin”. The only phone number is a tracking dummy. Case studies list industries without a method, a date range, or a data source. Then the business wonders why the site feels interchangeable.

Service search is also full of YMYL-adjacent queries even when the firm is not a hospital or a bank. Roofing after a storm, electrical work, legal intake, medical-adjacent clinics, and financial advice all sit closer to harm if the page is wrong. Google weights trust more heavily there. You do not need a legal practice to feel that pressure. You need a job where a bad hire costs money or safety.

## First-Hand Proof Beats Adjectives

First-hand proof is evidence that the writer or the firm touched the work. For a service business that usually looks like:

A named person describing a job they actually ran, with the constraint that made the job hard.
A photograph or screenshot from the work, not a stock image of a handshake.
A method: what was diagnosed, what was changed, what was measured, and what was left alone.
A limit: what the work did not cover, and what the data cannot prove.

On SEO accounts we treat Google Search Console, crawl data, ranking software with the engine and location stated, and enquiry events the business already tracks as evidence. Traffic by itself is not a win if the page is not generating the action the business wants. That rule is written into our [editorial standards](/editorial-standards/) because it is the same rule a rater is reaching for: is this write-up honest about what happened?

Stock photography of a smiling crew does not count as experience. A testimonial with a first name and a city that cannot be checked is weak. A “500% growth” claim with no tool, no date, and no definition of growth is not proof. It is advertising copy wearing a results costume.

If you cannot show the work, say less. A short, specific paragraph about one job is worth more than a grid of invented wins.

## Named Authors And Named Operators

Service sites hide people because they think the brand should be bigger than the staff. Search systems and buyers go the other way. They want a name they can search.

Put a real byline on advice. Give that person a profile with a photograph, a role, and a work history that matches what they write. If a junior writer drafted the post and a senior operator checked it, say so. Do not paste a founder name onto copy they never saw.

The about record should name the people who actually run the work. Ours lists Ben Lowe, Sassin Lowe, Jani A. Cañonero and Jewe O. Manalo because those are the people accountable for strategy, operations and delivery. If you want the same standard on your site, start from [who we are](/about-us/) and copy the structure, not the adjectives: names, roles, location, and a way to verify the company.

Anonymous “our team of experts” copy fails two tests at once. A rater cannot tell who created the page. A buyer cannot tell who will answer the phone.

## Contact That A Human Can Use

Trust collapses when the business cannot be reached. A form with no address, no hours, no legal name and no response path looks like a site that was built to collect leads and disappear.

Service businesses need a working phone or a monitored form, a physical location if they have one, and consistent NAP details across the site and Google Business Profile. Quezon City is on our public record because that is where the in-house team works. If you operate from a home office, say that honestly rather than renting a virtual suite you never visit.

Do not put a tracking number on the only public contact line if you cannot keep it live. A dead click-to-call is worse than a simple email.

If someone wants to check a claim, they should be able to [get in touch](/contact/) without hunting through a chatbot that never hands off to a person.

## Case Studies Without Theatre

Case studies are the service-business version of first-hand experience. They fail when they become a press release.

Write the problem, the diagnosis, the work, and the measurement. Name the market and the job type. Use a data source the client already has. Do not publish another company’s URL to win a pitch unless that business has given written permission. We do not name client websites by default for that reason. Proof is the method and the change in the account, not a screenshot of someone else’s domain.

Never invent a client, a quote, a ranking, a traffic number, a lead count or a revenue figure. If you do not have permission or a source, leave the number out. A rater who suspects fabrication will treat the whole site as low trust. Buyers who later compare your case study with their own Search Console will not hire you twice.

Guaranteed rankings and guaranteed AI Overview inclusion belong in the same bin. Those systems choose sources. You can improve eligibility. You cannot sell inclusion.

## What You Can Control On Your Own Site

You cannot assign yourself authoritativeness. You can make experience, expertise and trust visible.

Experience: original process writing, screenshots, methods, and named operators.
Expertise: pages that answer the actual job, not synonym-stuffed service clones.
Trust: identity, contact, legal pages, editorial rules, HTTPS, accurate claims, and corrections when you get a fact wrong.

Authoritativeness then has something to attach to. Other sites cite work that is specific. They do not cite a homepage that says world-class.

Internal consistency matters more than people admit. If the about copy says fifteen years, the author profile should not say ten. If the footer lists Quezon City, the schema and Google Business Profile should not list a different city. Search systems reconcile entities. Contradictions look like two businesses sharing a coat of paint.

## What Quality Raters Are Looking For

The Search Quality Rater Guidelines ask whether the page and the site appear to have a beneficial purpose, whether the content is created with people in mind, and whether the creator has the experience or expertise the topic needs. For a service query they also look at reputation and the information the site gives about itself.

That is why a beautiful service pitch with no owner, no method and no contact still fails. The rater is not scoring adjectives. They are asking: if a buyer hired this firm, would they know who they hired?

Read the guidelines as a self-check, not as a ranking cheat sheet. There is no form where you submit your E-E-A-T score. There is a human who can tell a real operator from a template.

## A Practical Build Order For A Service Site

Start with identity. Legal name, location, named people, and a working contact route.

Write each service URL around jobs you actually sell. One job per URL. Describe diagnosis, delivery and what the client has to provide. Cut the synonym clones.

Add proof only where you have it. One honest case is better than six inflated ones.

Give advice a byline and a profile. If nobody on staff can stand behind the post, do not publish it.

Publish the rules you write by: what you will claim, what you will not, and how you correct mistakes.

Then maintain it. A trust record that goes stale is worse than a short, current one.

## How We Apply This To Our Own Work

We run a Philippines SEO agency, so we are a service business making the same claims we ask clients to make. Strategy sits with Ben Lowe. Delivery sits with the in-house team in Quezon City. Public pages carry named authors. Recommendations go through senior review before they are treated as ready to implement. Reporting uses Search Console, crawls, ranking tools with the engine and location stated, and enquiry events the business already tracks.

We will not fill a tracking gap with made-up conversions. We will not mint thin city pages for offices we do not have. We will not publish a separate thin URL for GEO or ChatGPT SEO when the work belongs on the existing AI search service.

That is not a philosophy poster. It is the minimum a service firm should be able to show if it wants search systems and buyers to treat it as real.

If your site still reads like a brochure, fix identity, proof and contact before you commission another round of blog posts. Advice from an unnamed expert on a firm nobody can reach does not become trustworthy because the word count went up.
