Local businesses want new Google reviews on a regular rhythm. That rhythm is review velocity. The cheap way to fake it is bought stars, staff phones, and “5-star only” posters. That way is also how profiles get reviews unpublished, new reviews blocked, or a warning shown to customers. Google’s Fake Engagement rules are public. We follow them.

We help clients build a request habit after real jobs. We do not write reviews, we do not pay for them, we do not filter by star rating, and we do not run devices or emulators to look like many customers. If a vendor offers a package of Google reviews, the answer is no.

## Why A Steady Flow Beats A One-Off Dump

A profile that jumps from 4 reviews to 80 in a week, all written in similar English, all five stars, all from accounts with no photos, does not look like a busy shop. It looks like a project. Unusual volumes and patterns are exactly the kind of thing Google lists under rating manipulation.

A profile that gains a few genuine reviews each month, in the mix of languages your customers actually speak, with specific details about the job, looks like a business that is open. Velocity in that sense is a byproduct of completed work plus a simple ask. It is not a quota we assign to staff as “get 10 fives this week.” Google’s current prohibited content language even flags merchants requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews, and requesting that reviews include specific content.

Old reviews still help social proof, but recency matters to readers. A 4.8 from 2019 with nothing this year reads abandoned. Asking today’s customers is how the date on the most recent review stays current. That is the only velocity we care about.

Reviews also feed [Google Business Profile and local pack work](/blog/google-business-profile-and-local-pack-work-in-the-philippines/). They are not the whole prominence story, and they are not a reason to break policy.

## What Google Does Not Allow

Google’s prohibited and restricted content policies say contributions should reflect a genuine experience. Fake engagement is not allowed. That includes content not based on a real experience, reviews or ratings that have been paid for directly or in kind, content posted from multiple accounts by or at the request of one person, and content posted with emulators or other tricks to mimic genuine engagement.

Merchants are not allowed to solicit or encourage content that does not represent a genuine experience. They are not allowed to offer incentives such as payment, discounts, free goods, or services in exchange for a review, or for changing or removing a negative review. They are not allowed to post on a competitor’s listing to undermine it.

Rating manipulation also covers incentivised or biased reviews, conflict of interest (staff, family, contractors, competitors), unusual volumes or patterns, and selectively soliciting positive reviews or discouraging negative ones. When asking, merchants should not require or pressure people to leave ratings while on the premises, and should not request that specific content be included.

Google may restrict a profile that violates Fake Engagement: blocking new reviews for a time, unpublishing existing ones, or showing a warning that fake reviews were removed. Appeals exist. Prevention is cheaper.

We treat those paragraphs as the brief. A “free dessert for a Google review” is an incentive. A QR code at the till that says “leave us 5 stars” is selective solicitation. A staff group chat that says everyone must review from a second Gmail is multi-account fake engagement. None of that is a grey area we will “test.”

## What Google Does Allow

Google does allow merchants to solicit or encourage content that represents a genuine experience, without offering incentives and without trying to influence the rating or the contents of the review. That is the whole legal ask: if you actually did the job, you may invite the customer to say so on Google, with no reward and no script that demands stars or phrases.

A message after the visit, a link or QR that goes to the public review form, and a verbal “if you have a minute, you can review us on Google” are the allowed pattern, as long as they are not pressuring people on the spot and not filtering who gets asked based on mood. Asking only the customers who smiled, or handing a card only after a compliment, is selective solicitation. Ask from completed jobs as a process, not from a vibe.

You may respond to every review, including the bad ones. You may not offer a discount to take a bad review down. You may not tell people negative reviews are forbidden. You may report reviews that violate Google’s policies. You may not report them only because you dislike the score. Google says they do not get involved in business-versus-customer disputes, and that negative reviews are not automatically a sign of poor service.

## Build The Ask Into The Job, Not Into A Campaign

Velocity comes from volume of finished work times the percentage of people who are asked times the percentage who follow through. Most Philippine SMEs skip the ask entirely, then panic and buy reviews. We fix the ask.

The owner or the person who closed the job should send the invitation, because it feels like part of the service. A receptionist reading a script at the door, while the customer is still in the chair, is the pressure Google does not want. For a clinic, the follow-up SMS after the appointment works better than a laminated 5-star standee. For a trade, the message after the technician leaves, with the Google review link, is enough.

Put the link where staff will actually use it: the invoice email, the SMS template, the job-completion screen in your booking tool. Do not hide it, and do not surround it with “only if you had a great experience.” That sentence is gating.

Do not require a Google login tutorial in person. People who want to review already have Google accounts. People who do not will not become reviewers because you hovered. Offer the link and stop.

If you have several branches, each customer should review the location they used. Sending everyone to the head office profile is how branch listings starve. Sending them to a Facebook recommendation instead of Google is your choice, but it does not help Maps.

## Respond Without Coaching The Next Review

Public replies are for the next reader as much as for the reviewer. Thank people for specifics. If something went wrong, say what you will check. Do not write a legal brief. Do not accuse the customer of lying in public. Do not end every reply with “please update your review to 5 stars.” That is asking for a revision, which sits next to the incentive rules we already will not touch.

Never promise a refund or a free job in the Google reply in exchange for an edit. Handle make-goods off the thread. If the review violates policy (spam, fake, conflict of interest, harassment), use Google’s report flow. If it is merely negative, it stays.

Staff should not reply from personal accounts as if they were customers. Staff should not review their own workplace. Family and contractors are conflict of interest. We would rather have fewer reviews than a cluster of first-name-only fives from the same barangay on the same afternoon.

## Volume, Language, And Usefulness

A useful review names the service, the place, and what happened. You cannot demand that text. You can do work people can describe. Clear invoices, named technicians, and photos of the finished job on your own profile give customers something to remember. That is operations, not review gating.

English, Tagalog, and mixed reviews are all fine. Do not translate someone’s review for them. Do not ask them to rewrite in English for “SEO.” Google and Filipino searchers can read both. Scraped identical paragraphs in polished English are a spam smell.

Photos from customers help, but only if they took them. Do not send a customer your marketing photo and ask them to upload it as a review photo. Do not review-bomb yourself with staff uploads.

If velocity is zero, the cause is usually that nobody asks, the link is wrong, or the jobs are not finishing cleanly enough that anyone would want to speak. Buying stars will not repair a messy job. It will add policy risk on top of the messy job.

## Attacks, Fakes Aimed At You, And Restrictions

Competitors and angry people sometimes leave false reviews. Google’s process is to report policy-violating reviews, not to hire a retaliation farm. We document the pattern, report each qualifying review, and appeal restrictions if Google applies them in error. We do not post on the competitor’s profile to “even it out.” That is the undermining behaviour Google already forbids.

If Google restricts the profile, follow their email and the appeal path. Do not create a second listing to escape the restriction. Do not keep buying reviews during an appeal. Stop the behaviour, then appeal.

Extortion scams that threaten negative reviews have a separate reporting path in Google’s Help. Pay the scammer and you teach them the business works. We will not negotiate that for you.
